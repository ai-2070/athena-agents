# The tells, by where they show

The catalogue runs in the order an editor reads: the first and last lines, then sentence by sentence, then a stretch
of paragraphs at once, then the page as a whole. Many of the surest tells sit at the edges of a text and of its
paragraphs, so a pass over the openings and endings alone finds a good share of them.

Each entry gives what the tell looks like, why it reads as generated and when to leave it, and one before and after.
Every after uses only what its before says; where the before has nothing underneath, the after is shorter and a note
says what to ask the writer. An entry marked "weak alone" counts only when other tells share the passage, because
careful writers make that choice on purpose. The rest are worth an edit on one sighting.

## First and last lines

A model opens by getting ready to answer and closes by summing up and offering more. Read the first and last sentence
of the text, and of each paragraph, on their own: when the text says the same without one, it goes.

### The chat wrapper

Looks like: "Great question!", "You're absolutely right", "Certainly!", "Here's a revised version:", "Here is a summary
of…" at the top; "Want me to…?", "Happy to adjust the tone", "Feel free to reach out", "I hope this helps" at the
bottom.

The reply to a chat request is still wrapped around text meant to stand on its own. No tell is surer, and it hides
easily when real content sits inside it. Take off the wrapper and keep what it held.

> Before: Absolutely! Here's the summary you asked for. The team agreed to move the launch to June. Want me to turn
> this into an email for the client?
>
> After: The team agreed to move the launch to June.

### Run-ups

Looks like: "Here's the thing", "Let's dive in", "Honestly?", "Here's what you need to know", "Real talk", "Let's
break it down", "So," opening a piece; a question answered in the next breath ("Why does this matter? Because…"); an
opener that sympathises before saying anything ("We know mornings can be hectic", "Let's face it", "Finding time to
cook isn't always easy").

Each promises a point, or acts out candour or sympathy, where the sentence could make the point. "Honestly" in the
middle of an ordinary sentence is how people talk; the tell is the word standing alone at the front. A mild run-up
("isn't always easy") is still a run-up. Keep a question the text really leaves open, and keep the writer's own
colourful words when they carry meaning, as "before the good stuff goes" does here.

> Before: Let's face it: getting to the market before the good stuff goes isn't always easy. That's why you can now
> text us your order.
>
> After: You can now text us your order, so you don't have to get to the market before the good stuff goes.

### Writing about the text

Looks like: "In this article, we'll explore", "This section covers", "As mentioned above", "The table below shows"
(with the table in plain view), a first sentence that repeats the heading above it, "The new parser replaces the
regex-based one we used before", "compiled from publicly available sources".

The text describes itself (its plan, its history, how it was put together) and the subject waits. Start with the
subject. History belongs in change logs, release notes and migration guides, where change is the subject. Keep a
credit the reader can follow to a source, a warning that changes what they'd do, and the short roadmap a long report or
paper is expected to open with.

> Before: In this guide, we will explore how to back up a project. The steps below are listed in the order you should
> follow them.
>
> After: To back up a project: (and then the steps)

### Morals, kickers and send-offs

Looks like: a last line that says the paragraph again ("That's the power of good architecture."); "And that changes
everything.", "Let that sink in.", "Read that again."; a sentence after an example naming what it showed ("This is why
testing matters."); a run of fragments for effect ("No account. No config. Just results."); a word in capitals or cut
up with full stops ("Every. Single. Time."). At the end of a piece: "Exciting times lie ahead", "This is just the
beginning", "The future looks bright", "Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive", or a closing section in a
stock shape ("Challenges and future outlook").

Such a line has the reader admire a point already made, or leaves them with a mood, and says nothing new. A short
last sentence earns its place when it carries a fact or consequence the paragraph didn't. Cut the rest, and rebuild
fragments as one sentence that has a subject. A piece ends on the last fact it can show, or on real plans if it states
them.

> Before: We moved the nightly import onto a queue. Imports that used to fail at 2 a.m. now retry on their own. That's
> the power of good architecture.
>
> After: We moved the nightly import onto a queue. Imports that used to fail at 2 a.m. now retry on their own.

## Sentence by sentence

Phrases that make a plain statement sound bigger, deeper or better sourced than it is. A real fact usually sits under
the phrase: keep the fact and drop what's wrapped round it.

### A contrast with a claim nobody made

Looks like: "it's not X, it's Y"; "not X but Y"; "not just (only, merely) X, but Y"; "Y rather than X"; "less X than
Y"; the same move over two sentences ("This isn't about X. It's about Y."); a denial tacked on the end ("…, no sign-up
needed"). Other languages have their own form of it.

Denying something no one said makes the real point sound larger. Keep a contrast when the reader really holds the
first belief, or when each half is news ("It isn't the network. The nightly backup is still running at 9 a.m.").

> Before: Offline mode isn't just a feature; it's a promise that your work is always there.
>
> After: Offline mode keeps your work available without a connection.

### Sayings that sound deep

Looks like: "At its core", "The real question is", "fundamentally", "What really matters", "X is the new Y", "X is
the language (currency, architecture) of Y", "the heart of the matter", "X isn't a tool, it's a mirror".

A plain point is dressed as a proverb or a secret. Write the claim under the saying; if there isn't one, cut it.

> Before: At its core, onboarding is the architecture of trust. The real question is whether new users feel safe on
> day one.
>
> After: The test of onboarding is whether new users feel safe on their first day.

### Meaning hung on a fact

Looks like: "marks a pivotal moment", "a testament to", "plays a key (vital, crucial) role", "underscores the
importance of", "reflects a broader trend", "sets the stage for", "in today's fast-paced world", "the evolving
landscape"; a rider on the end of a fact: ", highlighting…", ", underscoring…", ", reflecting…", ", showcasing…",
", ensuring…", ", fostering…".

An ordinary fact is made to stand for something larger, or given a motive the text never states. Keep the fact. Keep
the meaning only where the text gives a reason for it (users asked for the feature; the move cut costs by an amount
the text names).

> Before: In March the team moved its tests to the new runner, marking a pivotal moment in its journey toward
> engineering excellence and highlighting its commitment to quality.
>
> After: In March the team moved its tests to the new runner.

### Borrowed authority

Looks like: "experts say", "studies show", "research suggests", "many users feel", "critics argue", "it is widely
believed"; a list of outlets standing in for what they said ("featured in Forbes, Wired and The Guardian"); follower
counts offered as proof.

Someone unnamed holds up the claim, or a row of names holds up a person. If the text names the source and what it
found, use that. If it doesn't, don't supply one: drop the authority and ask whether a source exists. Most writing
cites nothing, so a missing citation alone isn't a tell.

> Before: Studies show that short daily standups improve productivity for remote teams.
>
> After: Short daily standups improve productivity for remote teams. (Ask which study. With one, name it and what it
> measured; without one, the sentence is now the writer's own claim, and they may want it softer.)

### Sales words, and jargon the speaker wouldn't use

Looks like: seamless, powerful, robust (outside engineering), cutting-edge, game-changing, revolutionary, effortless,
best-in-class, elevate, unlock, empower, supercharge, stunning, breathtaking, nestled, vibrant, must-have; and a product
team's words in someone else's mouth: a shop offering "real-time updates", an "ordering experience", a "platform", a
"solution", a "curated selection".

Adjectives stand in for what the thing does, and the vocabulary is a pitch deck's. Copy meant to sell keeps selling,
in concrete terms and in the speaker's own words: say what the reader gets, the way the reader would say it. A
technical sense ("a robust estimator") isn't a tell.

> Before: Our new app delivers real-time stock updates and a seamless ordering experience for all your favourite
> cheeses.
>
> After: With our new app you can see what's in stock right now and order your favourite cheeses.

### Talking to everyone at once

Looks like: "Whether you're a beginner or a pro", "whether you're X or Y", "from startups to enterprises", "for
everyone from students to CEOs", "no matter your…".

The sentence names two kinds of reader to cover them all, and speaks to neither. Talk to the one reading, as "you",
and say what's in it for them. Keep it when the text really treats the two groups differently and says how.

> Before: Whether you're a morning regular or a weekend visitor, you can now order ahead on our website.
>
> After: You can now order ahead on our website.

### The long way round

Looks like: "stands as", "serves as", "acts as", "represents" where "is" would do; "boasts", "features", "offers"
for "has"; "is designed to" when it simply does; "associated with", "linked to", "in connection with" when the text
knows how the two are related; "in order to", "due to the fact that", "at this point in time", "has the ability to",
"it is important to note that".

Grand or roundabout words take the place of plain ones, and vague ones hide a relation the text knows. Use "is",
"has", "to", "because", "now" and "can", and name the relation the text gives ("founded", "works for"). Where the text
itself is vague, stay vague: a guessed role is worse. "It is important to note that" goes, and the sentence says the
thing.

> Before: The settings page serves as the central hub for configuration and boasts over forty options. In order to
> change the theme, open Appearance.
>
> After: The settings page is where you configure the app, and it has over forty options. To change the theme, open
> Appearance.

### Words models overuse (in clusters)

Looks like, several to a passage: delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, intricate, multifaceted, nuanced, realm,
landscape (for a field), interplay, underscore, showcase, foster, leverage (as a verb), harness, navigate (a problem),
embark, bolster, garner, meticulous, additionally, moreover, furthermore.

One formal word means nothing, and literal or technical senses stay (leverage in finance, a landscape photograph).
When this list looks dated, Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" page keeps a current one, sorted by when each word was
in fashion.

> Before: Additionally, the report delves into the intricate interplay between pricing and churn, underscoring the
> pivotal role of onboarding.
>
> After: The report also looks at how pricing and churn affect each other, and finds that onboarding matters a great
> deal.

### A gap admitted, then filled with a guess

Looks like: "as of my last update", "as of my knowledge cutoff", "not publicly available", "while specific details
are limited", "it is believed that", "based on available information"; "likely" or "probably" filling a gap the text
has just owned up to.

The text says it doesn't know, then guesses in words that make the guess sound likely. Say plainly what the sources
don't show, or cut the sentence; the guess goes either way.

> Before: While details about the company's early years are scarce, it was likely founded by engineers who wanted to
> simplify payroll.
>
> After: The sources say little about the company's early years.

### Stacked hedges (weak alone)

Looks like: "could potentially", "may possibly", "it could be argued that", "in some cases it may", "arguably", "to
some extent"; two or more qualifiers on one claim.

Hedges pile up when a draft is patched to soften an overclaim, more often than from any particular doubt. Keep one
qualifier where the doubt is real: "This change could potentially help to reduce some of the load in certain cases"
becomes "This change may reduce the load in some cases". "Usually" and "tends to" are how people write.

### Hidden actors (weak alone)

Looks like: passives that hide who acted ("mistakes were made"), and sentences with no subject ("No login needed.").

Name the actor when the text says who it is: "The report was reviewed by the finance team" becomes "The finance team
reviewed the report". When the text doesn't say, the passive may be the honest choice; don't guess an actor.

## Across paragraphs

Some tells pass every sentence-level check and show only when you read a stretch at once. Read for them separately,
after the sentence edits.

### Answering an objection nobody raised

Looks like: "To be clear", "I'm not saying", "Don't get me wrong", "This isn't to say", "You might think… but", "Some
would argue", "A tempting approach would be", "It would be easy to just"; several options raised and turned down in a
row.

It's usually a remnant of an earlier version. Drop the defence and keep any claim inside it. An objection the text
pins on a real person and answers stays, and so does an option a reader would really weigh.

> Before: To be clear, I'm not saying the old system was bad. You might think we should just add more servers, but the
> problem is the database locks.
>
> After: The problem is the database locks. (If someone really did propose more servers: "More servers won't help,
> because the problem is the database locks.")

### Threes by habit

Looks like: three adjectives, three verbs or three parallel sentences, again and again, often where the content has
two things or four; three quick examples and then a lesson.

Three sounds complete, so ideas get padded or trimmed to fit. Check what each item adds: a vague one goes, and the
writer is asked what it meant. Items the text really has are content, however many there are: "browse, reserve and
collect" names three things a site does, and all three stay.

> Before: The new dashboard is faster, smarter and more intuitive.
>
> After: The new dashboard is faster. (Ask what "smarter" and "more intuitive" mean here; each answer is worth a
> sentence. Until then they go, and the writer is told.)

### The same opening, sentence after sentence

Looks like: several sentences in a row that start with the same subject ("The script… The script… The script…").

Join them ("The script reads the log, finds the failed jobs and retries each one"), give one a different subject, or
open with the verb. A writer may repeat an opening for its rhythm, and numbered steps can each start with a verb;
leave those.

### Every paragraph built the same way

Looks like: each paragraph opening on a question, or closing on a moral, or making its point with three examples;
sections of near-equal length whatever they hold; the same connector at every turn ("Additionally", "Moreover").

A template shows when it repeats. Fix each instance with the entries above, then let each paragraph take the shape its
content needs: long where there's more to say, short where there isn't.

### Rebuilding what the reader already knows

Looks like, in a reply: the other person's problem recapped; the diagnosis walked through step by step; proof that the
plan works; and the answer in the last line.

Models assume a reader who knows nothing, and the reader of a reply knows plenty. Each sentence can be fine, so this
survives sentence edits. Lead with the answer and keep what the reader lacks: usually one fact, plus anything they need
in order to act. Act on it when you can see the thread or the text is plainly a reply; otherwise leave it.

> Before (to the person who reported the bug): Thanks for flagging this! To recap, the export fails when a project name
> contains a slash. I looked into it and confirmed the slash is read as a folder separator, which is the cause. I've
> now fixed it by replacing slashes in the file name, and I've added a test.
>
> After: Thanks, fixed. The slash in the name was being read as a folder separator, so the export now replaces slashes
> in the file name. There's a test for it.

### Vouching in place of reporting

Looks like, mostly in PR descriptions, commit messages and status updates: "carefully reviewed", "comprehensive",
"ensured backward compatibility", "following best practices", "robust error handling", "preserved all existing
functionality", "production-ready", "thoroughly tested".

The writer vouches for their own care and leaves out what they did and how they checked it. Say what changed and what
was checked; a claim nobody checked comes out. This is the one to watch in your own reports.

> Before: This PR comprehensively refactors the auth module following best practices, ensuring robust error handling
> and full backward compatibility. All tests pass.
>
> After: Refactors the auth module. All tests pass. (Then ask, or answer from the work if the PR is yours: what changed
> in error handling, and how was compatibility checked? Each answer is a sentence worth adding.)

## On the page

What shows before you read a word. Templates and editors make tidy formatting too, so formatting proves little by
itself; the giveaway is the same decoration on every item, or marks left from wherever the text was drafted.

### Placeholders and paste debris

Looks like: "[Your name]", "[Insert date]", "2025-XX-XX", "INSERT_LINK_HERE", "Lorem ipsum", "Note: replace this with
your own example"; citation markers such as `contentReference[oaicite:0]`; a link tagged `utm_source=chatgpt.com` or
another chatbot's name; the whole text inside a code fence marked `markdown`.

Fill a placeholder from what the user gave you, or ask. Don't guess a name, date or link: a wrong one sent to a client
is worse than a visible blank. Citation markers come out. A tracking tag can come off a link without changing where it
goes; in a file, say you removed it.

> Before: Hi [Client Name], following up on our call on [date]. The proposal is attached.
>
> After: ask for the name and the date, and until you have them leave the brackets where the writer will see them.

### Bold on everything

Looks like: bold on phrases with no reason to stand out; a list whose items each start with a bolded label and colon,
the label repeating what follows.

Take the bold off. When the labels add nothing, the list can become a sentence or a plain list. Keep bold where a
reader scans for it: a warning, a term defined once.

> Before:
>
> - **Speed:** Pages load faster thanks to caching.
> - **Reliability:** Uploads retry when the connection drops.
>
> After: Pages load faster thanks to caching, and uploads retry when the connection drops.

### Dressed-up headings

Looks like: title case where the house style is sentence case; emoji or arrows in headings; a heading meant to
intrigue ("The secret sauce") where a plain one would serve ("How caching works"); a rule between every section; a top
heading that repeats the document's title.

Follow the house style where there is one; many publications use title case on purpose. Otherwise use sentence case,
drop the decoration, and name what the section holds: "🚀 Getting Started: Your Journey Begins Here" becomes "Getting
started".

### Markdown where it won't render

Looks like: asterisks, pound signs and hyphen bullets in an email, a Word document, a text message, a form field or
anywhere else that shows them as typed.

The text was written in a chat window and pasted somewhere else. Write it for where it's going: in a plain email, "Two
things to do: send the budget and book the room" can replace a bold heading over two starred bullets.

### Dashes everywhere (weak alone)

Looks like: em dashes (—), en dashes (–) or a spaced double hyphen (--) joining clauses, several to a paragraph.

A dash spares the writer from deciding how two ideas relate. Plenty of good writers use them, so one means nothing
while a page full of them means something. Choose the mark the relation needs: a full stop, a comma, a colon,
brackets. Leave dashes in ranges (pages 10–12), code, commands and URLs.

> Before: The migration — which took three weekends — moved every customer to the new database — without a single
> outage.
>
> After: The migration took three weekends and moved every customer to the new database without a single outage.

### Curly quotes (weak alone)

Looks like: curly quotes (“ ” ‘ ’) in a text that otherwise uses straight ones, or the reverse.

Word processors curl quotes by themselves, so this means little alone. Match the rest of the text: straight in
Markdown source, code and plain text, and elsewhere whatever the house style uses.

### Hyphens (weak alone)

A compound before a noun takes a hyphen ("a short-term fix"); after the noun, style guides differ, so follow the house
style. An -ly adverb takes none ("a highly visible bug"), and words the dictionary hyphenates ("third-party") keep it.
Fix the wrong ones; as evidence they're weak either way.

## A worked example

A bookshop's post, pasted with "make this sound less like AI":

> 📚 Exciting News: Introducing Our Brand-New Online Ordering Experience! 📚
>
> We're thrilled to announce that Hartley Books now has a seamless online ordering platform — a game-changer for book
> lovers everywhere. We know that finding time to visit isn't always easy. Whether you're a busy parent or a lifelong
> reader, our platform empowers you to:
>
> - **Browse** our curated shelves from the comfort of your home
> - **Reserve** titles with real-time stock updates
> - **Collect** your order in store at your convenience
>
> This isn't just a website — it's a celebration of our community. We can't wait to see you!

The reply. The rewrite comes first, in its own quote block:

> You can now order from Hartley Books online.
>
> Browse our shelves from home and reserve the books you'd like: the site shows what's in stock right now. Then
> collect them from the shop whenever it suits you.
>
> We can't wait to see you!

Then a few lines for the writer, in plain sentences:

The headline, emoji and bold labels went, along with "seamless", "game-changer", "empowers" and "curated", and
"real-time stock updates" now says what a customer sees. The sympathy opener, the "whether you're" line and "This isn't
just a website" came out, since none of them said anything about the shop.

I kept the news as the first line, all three things the site lets you do, and "we", "our shelves" and the sign-off,
because this is the shop talking to its customers.

What's the web address? It belongs in the first line. If you'd like the post livelier, a real detail would do it, such
as "We'll keep a reserved book for [how long you hold reservations]". Tell me what goes in the brackets, or leave that
line out.
